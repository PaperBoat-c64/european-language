# Dutch — Teaching Norm

Load when `textLanguage` is `nl`. Dutch has two genders, verb-second main clauses with verb-final subordinate clauses, separable verbs, and a spoken-written split in past-tense use. Do not copy German noun-capitalization or case rules.

## 1. Orthography and Unicode

- The native alphabet has no diacritics. Accents appear in loanwords (`café`, `crèche`, `déjà vu`) and as a stress/emphasis device on `é`/`è` (`één` "one" vs `een` "a"; `hé`). Preserve; do not add or strip.
- `ij` is a digraph, often treated as one unit: capitalized together at a word start (`IJsland`, `IJssel`), and sometimes written as the ligature `ĳ` (U+0133). Preserve the source's form; do not transcribe `ij` as `y`.
- No noun capitalization: common nouns are lowercase (`de man`, `het huis`). Capitalize proper nouns, sentence starts, and the formal `U` ("you").
- Trema (diaeresis) marks vowel hiatus, not vowel quality: `geëerd`, `zeeën`, `reëel`, `ideeën`, `ruïne`. Keep it.
- Apostrophes: genitive after sibilants and vowel-final names (`Hans' boek`, `Anna's fiets`); frozen genitive remnants (`'s morgens` = "in the morning"); contractions (`m'n`, `z'n`, `'t`).
- Quotation marks: `" "` or `„ "`. Preserve the source's style.

## 2. Segmentation

Split at `.` `?` `!` and `…`. Do not split after:

- abbreviations: `dhr.`, `mevr.`, `dr.`, `prof.`, `bijv.`, `d.w.z.`, `enz.`, `t.a.v.`, `m.b.t.`, `ca.`, `nr.`;
- numbers: `3,14`, `1.000,50`, `10.30 uur`;
- initials.

Dutch sentences with long verb-final subordinate clauses can run long; prefer the larger unit when the boundary is uncertain.

## 3. Nouns, gender, number, articles

- Two genders: common (`de`) and neuter (`het`). Gender is lexical and mostly not inferable from meaning; compounds take the gender of the last element (`de trein` + `het kaartje` → `het treinkaartje`).
- Definite article: `de` (common) / `het` (neuter); indefinite: `een`. There is no indefinite plural article — use the bare noun (`ik zie huizen` = "I see houses").
- Plurals: `-en` (`boek → boeken`), `-s` (`tafel → tafels`; `-s` with apostrophe after a long vowel: `auto → auto's`, `foto → foto's`), vowel/shortening changes (`stad → steden`, `schip → schepen`, `dag → dagen`), irregular `-eren` (`kind → kinderen`, `ei → eieren`), borrowed `-a`/`-i` (`museum → musea`).
- Diminutive: `-je` (`huisje`), `-tje` after long vowels and diphthongs (`autootje`, `maantje`), `-etje` after short-vowel sonorant-final stems (`mannetje`, `bloemetje`), `-pje` after `-m` (`boompje`), `-kje` after `-ng` (`koninkje`). Diminutives are productive and carry smallness or affection.
- Adjective before a noun takes `-e` (`de grote man`, `een grote man`) except after `een`/`geen`/a possessive with a `het`-word (`een groot huis`, but `het grote huis`, `mijn grote huis`).
- No case system; frozen genitive remnants survive (`'s morgens`, `te allen tijde`, `Koninkrijk der Nederlanden`).

## 4. Verbs

- Weak, strong, and mixed verbs. Weak: `werken – werkte – gewerkt`. Strong (vowel change): `zingen – zong – gezongen`, `lopen – liep – gelopen`, `drinken – dronk – gedronken`. Mixed (weak ending, strong vowel): `brengen – bracht – gebracht`, `denken – dacht – gedacht`, `kopen – kocht – gekocht`.
- Auxiliaries: `hebben` for most verbs; `zijn` for a set of intransitive motion/change/state verbs (`komen, gaan, blijven, gebeuren, worden, zijn, sterven, vallen, groeien`). State the auxiliary with each perfect.
- Past participle: weak `ge-…-d`/`-t` (spelling: a stem ending in a consonant of `'t kofschip` — t, k, f, s, ch, p — takes `-t`, otherwise `-d`: `gewerkt`, `gespeeld`); strong `ge-…-en` (`gezongen`); verbs with unstressed prefixes `be-, ge-, er-, her-, ont-, ver-` take no `ge-` (`besteld`, `verteld`, `herkend`).
- Separable verbs: the stressed prepositional prefix moves to the clause end in main clauses (`opstaan → ik sta om zeven uur op`), stays attached in subordinate clauses and with modals (`…dat ik om zeven uur opsta`; `ik wil opstaan`), and reattaches in the participle (`opgestaan`).
- Tenses: present (also used with a time expression for future: `morgen ga ik`); simple past for written narrative; present perfect for past in speech. Both map onto English simple past/present perfect differently, so explain by register and narrative function, not by English form.
- Future: `zullen + inf` (neutral), `gaan + inf` (intention/imminent), present + time expression. Conditional: `zouden + inf`.
- Progressive: `aan het + infinitive` (`ik ben aan het lezen`).
- Modals: `kunnen, moeten, mogen, willen, zullen, hoeven (te)`; `hoeven … te` = "need not" and takes `te`.
- Present spelling: 1sg is the bare stem, 2/3sg add `-t` (`ik loop, jij loopt, hij loopt`), but the `-t` drops after `je`/`jij` in inversion (`loop je?`).

## 5. Word order and pronouns

- Main clause V2: the finite verb is second; a non-subject element in first position triggers inversion (`Ik koop morgen brood` / `Morgen koop ik brood`). Coordinate conjunctions `en, maar, want, of, dus` do not invert.
- Subordinate clause: verb-final (SOV) after `dat, omdat, hoewel, toen, als, terwijl, zodat, voordat, nadat, die, dat` (`…omdat ik morgen brood koop`).
- Separable bracket: the prefix sits at the clause end, forming a bracket with the finite verb (`ik sta … op`).
- Negation: `geen` negates an indefinite noun (`ik heb geen geld` = "I have no money"); `niet` negates verbs, adjectives, definite/pronoun objects, and prepositional phrases (`ik kom niet`, `ik heb het geld niet`). `geen` = `niet een`.
- Subject pronouns and reduced forms: `ik, jij/je, hij/ie, zij/ze, het/'t, wij/we, jullie, zij/ze`, formal `u`. Object: `mij/me, jou/je, hem/'m, haar/'r, het/'t, ons, jullie, hen/hun/ze`. Possessives: `mijn/m'n, jouw/je, zijn/z'n, haar/d'r, ons/onze, jullie, hun`. Reduced forms are unstressed and neutral, not slang.
- `er`: existential (`er is/er zijn`), partitive (`ik heb er twee`), pronominal adverb replacing `het/ze` after a preposition (`ik denk eraan`, `ik heb er geen zin in`), and locative (`er … heen`). Gloss by function.
- `het` dummy subject for weather/temperature/impersonal statements (`het regent`, `het is koud`); do not point it at a real referent.
- `die/dat`: demonstratives (`die man`, `dat huis`) and relative pronouns (`die` for de-words, `dat` for het-words).

## 6. Prepositions and valency

Record fixed verb/adjective-preposition patterns as valency:

- `denken aan`, `wachten op`, `houden van`, `praten over`, `zorgen voor`, `bestaan uit`, `afhangen van`, `luisteren naar`, `kijken naar`, `zoeken naar`;
- adjective patterns: `trots op`, `bang voor`, `boos op`, `tevreden met`, `dol op`;
- `aan het + inf` (progressive), `bezig met`;
- spoken contracted forms exist (`op 't`, `in d'n`), but standard writing keeps `op het`/`in de`.

## 7. Register and colloquial forms

- `jij/je` is unmarked/informal; `u` is formal/polite (capitalized `U` in letters). The choice is social, not grammatical; note it when the text turns on it.
- Informal contractions: `effe` (= `even`), `niet → nie`, `eens → 's` (`kijk 's`), dropped final `-t` and `-n` (`-en` rendered as `-n` in speech: `lop'n`). Mark `informal`, not errors.
- Colloquial lexis: `gast` (guy), `vet`/`gaaf` (cool), `doen` as a light verb (`dat doet 'm niet`), diminutives for warmth.
- Reduced `je`/`we`/`ze` are standard neutral speech; do not mark them substandard.

## 8. Regional variation

- Netherlands Dutch and Belgian (Flemish) Dutch differ. Flanders uses `gij/ge` for informal "you" with its own verb endings (`gij zijt`, `gij maakt`, `gij waart`); the Netherlands uses `jij/je`.
- Lexical differences: `goesting` (appetite/desire), `kot` (student room), `schoon` (beautiful in Flanders; clean in NL), `zetel` (seat/armchair in Flanders), `duimspijker` (thumbtack), `Ik zie u graag` (I love you, Flanders).
- Pronunciation differs (`g`/`ch` hard in the north, soft in the south) and surfaces in writing only in quoted speech.
- Label a word "Belgian Dutch" or "Netherlands Dutch" only with evidence from the source or a cited dictionary; otherwise mark `regionCertainty: "unverified"`. Frisian is a separate language, not a Dutch dialect.

## 9. Common learner errors and false friends

False friends (relative to English):

- `wil` = want (present of `willen`); English "will" is `zal/zullen`.
- `wie` = who; English "we" is `wij/we`.
- `wat` = what; `waar` = "where" AND "true" — read by syntax.
- `slim` = clever/smart; English "slim" is `dun`/`slank`.
- `als` = as/when/if (equality comparison); English "also" is `ook`; inequality comparison uses `dan`.
- `zee` = sea; English "see" is `zien` (verb) / `zicht` (noun).
- `brief` = letter; `map` = folder; `door` = through/by; `meer` = lake AND more; `dan` = than/then; `mogen` = may/be allowed (not "must" = `moeten`); `dom` = stupid.

Learner errors:

- V2 violations and missing inversion after a fronted adverbial; verb not final in subordinate clauses.
- Separable prefix left attached in main clauses (`*ik opsta`).
- `niet` vs `geen` confusion.
- Wrong `de`/`het` gender; wrong adjective ending after `een` + het-word (`*een grote huis`).
- `hebben` instead of `zijn` as perfect auxiliary.
- Participle `-d`/`-t` spelling; present-perfect vs simple-past register choice.
- `dan` vs `als` in comparisons (`hij is groter dan ik`, not `als`).

## 10. Teaching priorities for Dutch texts

1. Word order: V2, inversion, verb-final subordinate clauses.
2. Separable verbs and the clause bracket.
3. `niet`/`geen` and `er` in all its functions.
4. `de`/`het` gender and adjective endings.
5. Past-tense choice: simple past vs present perfect by register/narrative.
6. Reduced pronouns and the `jij`/`u` register choice.
